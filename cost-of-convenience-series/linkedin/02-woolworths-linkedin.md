# LinkedIn Promo — Part Two: Dear Amanda, What Are We Actually Trading?

## Image prompt (for Midjourney / DALL-E / Ideogram / Firefly)

Editorial photograph, documentary style. A cobblestone town square at
dawn, empty. Along the edges, market stalls stand folded and covered
under canvas tarps, as if a market used to happen here but hasn't in
some time — stacked wooden crates, a furled umbrella, chalk lettering
faded on an unused sign. Low morning mist sits over the stones. Warm
amber light catches one corner of the square; the rest fades into cool
blue-grey shadow. In the immediate foreground, slightly out of focus, a
single grocery delivery crate sits on a doorstep at the square's edge —
the only sign of the present day in an otherwise timeless scene. No
people, no faces, no logos, no text. Shot on a 35mm lens, shallow depth
of field, subtle film grain, in the style of a Bloomberg Businessweek or
The Economist cover photograph. Horizontal composition, generous
negative space in the upper third for a headline overlay. Mood: elegiac,
still, dignified — a place that used to gather, quietly not gathering.

**Spec:** 1600×840px (~1.91:1). Midjourney: `--ar 1.91:1 --style raw`.

**Backup prompt** (if the covered stalls read as generic "market" stock
imagery): narrow the frame to just one stall and one doorstep, in
tighter close-up — a single stacked crate of unopened vegetables under
canvas, next to a modern delivery crate still sealed with a plastic
courier bag. Same object, two eras, side by side.

## LinkedIn post copy

**Version A — story-first hook:**

> A few years ago I stood in a market square in rural France on a
> Saturday morning with no plan at all, and it was the most alive I'd
> felt in months.
>
> Nothing remarkable happened. A woman sold me cheese and asked how long
> I was staying. An old man argued with the fishmonger about a price
> neither of them actually cared about. Someone recognised someone else
> three stalls down and the whole morning paused for a hug.
>
> I hadn't felt that in Australia in years. Not because we don't have
> markets — because the default way we get food here stopped requiring
> a town to happen at all. And one company, more than any other, is the
> reason why. Not through anything sinister — just by doing its job
> better than almost anyone else in the country does theirs.
>
> Part Two of The Cost of Convenience is an open letter to Woolworths
> Group CEO Amanda Bardwell, asking the question I don't think anyone's
> asked her directly: what does "better at the job" cost the town once
> it stops needing you to walk into it?
>
> Read it here: [link]
>
> Comment WOOLIES if you think Amanda should see this one.

**Version B — provocation-first hook:**

> Every convenience we choose removes one more reason for a stranger to
> end up standing next to another stranger with nowhere to be for five
> minutes.
>
> We didn't mean to trade away the butcher who knew regulars by name. We
> just kept choosing the option that didn't require knowing anyone's
> name at all — and one day the butcher wasn't there to know it.
>
> None of that shows up on a balance sheet. GDP goes up every time
> convenience wins, because convenience is easy to measure and
> connection isn't. Nobody's ever built an index for how many unplanned
> conversations a suburb had this year.
>
> This week's letter in The Cost of Convenience goes straight to the
> person best placed to actually answer that: Woolworths Group CEO
> Amanda Bardwell. Not an attack — a genuine question about what we're
> trading for the gap between "I want it" and "it's in my hand" closing
> to zero.
>
> Part Two is live: [link]
>
> Comment WOOLIES if you want it to reach her.

Lead with **B** for the sharpest scroll-stop — it opens on the claim,
not the scene, and the "what shows up on a balance sheet" line tends to
travel well as a quote screenshot. **A** reads better if last week's
post skewed toward story and you want continuity of tone.

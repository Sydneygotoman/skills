# Lyric Writing

## Overview

This skill covers the full craft of songwriting — from first-line spark to polished draft. It handles original composition, targeted rewrites, genre adaptation, and structural analysis.

**Keywords**: lyrics, songwriting, hook, chorus, verse, bridge, rhyme, meter, syllable, melody, song structure, pop, rap, country, folk, R&B, rock, musical theatre, ballad, anthem

---

## Core Concepts

### Song Sections

| Section | Role |
|---|---|
| **Verse** | Advances the story or scene; each verse usually builds on the last |
| **Pre-Chorus** | Builds tension and energy leading into the chorus |
| **Chorus** | The emotional peak and main message; most repeated section |
| **Bridge** | Contrast and shift in perspective, key, or mood; appears once |
| **Outro/Coda** | Resolves or fades; can echo the hook in a new way |
| **Hook** | The single most memorable line or phrase (often the title) |

### Common Structures

- **Verse–Chorus**: V C V C B C (pop standard)
- **AABA**: Tin Pan Alley / jazz standard — two verse statements, a bridge, verse return
- **Through-composed**: No repeated sections; common in folk narratives and musical theatre
- **Verse only**: Story songs, rap, spoken word

## Craft Guidelines

### Rhyme

- Default to **end rhyme** (last stressed syllable of a line).
- Use **near rhyme / slant rhyme** freely — it sounds more natural than forced perfect rhyme.
- Common schemes: AABB (couplets), ABAB (alternating), ABCB (ballad), AAAB (terza rima variant).
- Avoid **inversion** ("the moon bright shone") and **forced padding** just to land a rhyme.
- Internal rhyme adds flow without constraining line endings.

### Meter and Syllable Count

- Match the natural stress of speech to the melodic stress of the song.
- When a melody is given or described, count syllables per line and match closely (±1–2 is usually fine).
- Common meters: iambic (da-DUM), trochaic (DUM-da), anapestic (da-da-DUM — common in country and folk).
- Choruses often have shorter, puncher lines than verses.

### Imagery and Specificity

- Concrete details outperform abstractions: *"a red '92 Civic with no AC"* beats *"an old car."*
- Lead with a strong opening image that drops the listener into a scene.
- Avoid lyric clichés unless used with ironic or deliberate intent: broken hearts, rain, fire metaphors, etc.

### The Hook

- The hook must be singable, memorable, and carry the song's emotional core.
- It is usually the title repeated or closely echoed.
- Aim for 4–8 syllables on the hook; shorter is often stronger.
- The hook should work stripped of the rest of the song — test it in isolation.

### Voice and Perspective

- **First person (I/we)**: intimate, confessional — pop, country, R&B.
- **Second person (you)**: direct address — creates tension or intimacy.
- **Third person (he/she/they)**: storytelling — folk, narrative ballads.
- Switching perspective mid-song can be powerful in a bridge.

### Emotional Arc

- A song should move — the emotional state at the end differs from the start.
- The bridge is the pivot: it reframes what came before or reveals something new.
- Resist resolving too early; let the tension breathe through the pre-chorus.

## Genre Conventions

### Pop
- Chorus arrives within 45–60 seconds.
- Hooks are short and title-forward.
- Post-chorus or drop is common for sonic release.
- Syllable density is moderate; clarity trumps complexity.

### Hip-Hop / Rap
- Rhyme density is high — multi-syllable and compound rhymes expected.
- Internal rhyme schemes define flow and style.
- Wordplay, puns, and double meanings add layers.
- 16-bar verse is standard; hook is 4–8 bars.

### Country
- Storytelling is paramount — character, setting, stakes within the first verse.
- Conversational diction; avoid anything that sounds staged.
- The twist or payoff in the final chorus is a genre trademark.
- Near rhyme is widely accepted.

### Folk / Singer-Songwriter
- Narrative detail and emotional honesty over polish.
- Through-composed structures work well.
- Repetition with variation (same words, evolved meaning) is effective.

### R&B / Soul
- Melisma-friendly lines — vowels that sustain naturally.
- Call-and-response between lead and backing vocals.
- Emotional vulnerability is a feature, not a flaw.

### Rock / Anthemic
- Short, declarative lines in the chorus ("We will rock you," "Don't stop believin'").
- Pre-chorus builds are crucial — the drop into the chorus must feel earned.
- Power words: action verbs, strong consonants.

### Musical Theatre
- Lyrics must carry dramatic subtext — characters sing what they cannot say.
- Verse often mirrors spoken dialogue rhythm.
- Rhyme is more formal than in pop; AABA is common.
- "I want" songs, "I am" songs, and "scene" songs are structural archetypes.

## Workflow

When writing or rewriting lyrics:

1. **Clarify inputs**: genre, mood, topic, perspective, any existing melody or syllable constraints, target length.
2. **Draft the hook first** — everything else serves it.
3. **Write the chorus** around the hook, establishing the emotional peak.
4. **Write verse 1** to set up the chorus — ground the listener in scene and character.
5. **Write verse 2** to advance or deepen the story (not repeat verse 1).
6. **Write the bridge** to pivot, contrast, or reveal — resist making it a third verse.
7. **Read aloud** — awkward stress patterns become obvious immediately.
8. **Check rhyme quality** — replace forced rhymes with slant rhymes or restructured lines.
9. **Present a clean draft** with section labels (VERSE 1, CHORUS, etc.).

When only a section is requested (e.g., "write a bridge"), apply the same quality bar to that section in isolation while keeping the rest of the song's voice consistent.

---

## Output Format

Present lyrics with clear section headers:

```
[VERSE 1]
...

[PRE-CHORUS]
...

[CHORUS]
...

[VERSE 2]
...

[BRIDGE]
...

[CHORUS]
...
```

After the draft, briefly note:
- Rhyme scheme used
- Any deliberate structural or stylistic choices
- Suggestions for alternate hooks or lines if the first draft might not fit the melody

## Examples

### Example 1 — Country Story Song Hook

Request: *Hook for a song about leaving a small town but missing it.*

Hook: *"The road got long but home got longer"*

Notes: 9 syllables, slant rhyme on "long/longer," contrasting motion vs. memory — classic country push-pull.

---

### Example 2 — Pop Chorus (ABAB rhyme, 8-syllable lines)

```
[CHORUS]
I keep the light on just in case    (A - 9)
you find your way back to my door   (B - 8)
I've memorized the empty space      (A - 9)
but I keep the light on for more    (B - 9)
```

Scheme: ABAB, near rhyme on "case/space," slight syllable variance (acceptable in pop).

---

### Example 3 — Hip-Hop Internal Rhyme

Request: *16-bar verse opening, introspective theme, multi-syllable rhymes.*

```
I been moving in silence, defying the climate
relying on science and timing, designing alignments
the grind is a diamond — I'm finding refinement
behind all the violence and sirens, I'm shining
```

Scheme: "-inding/-iming/-ining/-ining" chain across all four lines; internal rhyme reinforces flow.
